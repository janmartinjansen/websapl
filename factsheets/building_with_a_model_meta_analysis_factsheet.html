Building a Toolchain with a Model

In 18 weeks a lazy functional language got a self-hosting compiler, an optimiser, a typechecker, a REPL, and backends to bytecode, native code, WebAssembly, and Clean’s ABC. The generators at the end of the chain are small. This sheet asks why, based on the repository’s own history. Its answer is deliberately modest: three ingredients seem to matter together, and we cannot yet say which one matters most.

Status of the evidence. Everything below comes from this repository (git history, the bug reports in docs/, the measurements in the other factsheets) and from the author’s account of what was tried. It is a single project with a single human, so it generates hypotheses; it does not test them. Interpretation is marked as such. This page was itself drafted by a model, which is a conflict of interest worth stating.

3. The Hypothesis: Three Ingredients

A. An oracle

Something that says that a change is wrong: the gen1 fixpoint (check_bootstrap), byte-identical output between two generators, 13 benchmarks with known res: values, differential tests against GHC.

B. Readable intermediate stages

Something that says what is wrong: --emit=retag, the .jmvm text, stage dumps, the decompiler, difftrace.py, a trace viewer. Every stage has a form one can read and diff.

C. A human-chosen decomposition

The order of stages, the choice to keep each one minimal, and the decision to change the problem when an approach stalls. Supplied by the author, not by the model.

The ingredients play different roles in the evidence below: A governs how fast bugs surface, B how fast they are diagnosed, C whether hard steps become tractable at all. We could not separate their effects; there is no run without any of them.

What it supports

  • Fix latency was short; detection latency was not. Wherever the date is known, the fix came the day the bug was found. Latent periods ranged from hours to years and depended on whether some program exercised the construct.
  • Each new consumer found bugs. A lambda-calculus program, a resident REPL host, a REPL in Sapl, a Haskell front end, an ABC optimiser: each hit a construct the benchmarks never used. Breadth of use acted as the strongest debugger, as the author put it.
  • Readable stages shortened diagnosis (the jmpt report) and let the ABC bug be seen at all.

What it does not support

  • That readability finds bugs. Boundary bugs were found by growth and by AddressSanitizer, not by reading stages.
  • The dramatic reading of the 4 Aug case. Eight commits sounds like months of silent damage; it was one day. The lesson about tests that never recurse still stands.
  • Any causal claim. Nine hand-picked, documented bugs are a sample of the memorable ones; silently disappeared bugs are missing.

5. Evidence B: Four Tries at WebAssembly

The clearest contrast in the history, and the main support for ingredient C. From the TOP presentation and the WebAssembly factsheet:

The sequence on 20 November, from commit times: sapl2c to wasm at 10:05; browser measurements and an experiment on which clang level matters (11:00–13:30); the first direct Retag-to-wasm generator, in Python, at 14:19 and measured at 1.18× of emcc -O2 nine minutes later; the same generator in Sapl, byte-for-byte identical to the Python one, at 18:07.

What the C route did

  1. A working baseline that every later wasm module could be compared with.
  2. An instrument. Varying clang’s level showed that -O1 (variables in wasm locals) already matches -O2, and that wasm-opt cannot recover -O0. That named the mechanism the direct generator had to reproduce.
  3. A specification. “The C as its specification”: the direct generators were checked byte-for-byte against each other and against Emscripten’s result.

What actually changed

The starting point. From bytecode the wasm still plays stack machine; from Retag every variable becomes a wasm local. The earlier attempts were not wasted code but a different, harder problem. The same lesson had already appeared with jmvm2c (bytecode to C: 442 ms) against sapl2c (Retag to C: 244 ms).

Two things the log does not show. (1) The author recalls that the direct generator then took about half an hour; the log shows the first working commit 4 hours after the C route, with the experiments in between, and cannot tell how long the writing itself took. (2) The tokens spent on the earlier attempts are not recorded. Both would strengthen or weaken the argument and should be filled in by the author. Also note that the direct route is not finished: 3–6 of 14 benchmarks overflow the fixed browser call stack unless JSPI or an own stack is used.

7. A Test We Have Not Run

The strong form of the question: could a model, given a fixed oracle and a constraint such as “a Haskell implementation in under 10,000 lines”, produce something comparable with little steering and a lot of compute? We think the correctness part is plausible, because oracles exist (the Haskell report tests, differential testing against GHC). We doubt that the line limit alone yields an elegant design: it can be met with dense code or one large phase, and elegance is hard to score. A fair experiment would fix:

  • the oracle: report tests, differential output against GHC, the 12 benchmarks with known results;
  • a structural requirement: each phase must emit a readable intermediate form, with a line budget per phase, not only in total;
  • a measurement of speed against the existing toolchain;
  • and then record which decomposition the model chooses. If it finds a small first-order intermediate form by itself, much of what we attribute to ingredient C was less specific to us than we think. If it builds a large monolithic compiler that is correct, the human contribution was in the design.

8. Limits of This Analysis

  • One project, one human, one domain. A compiler has unusually good oracles; many tasks do not.
  • No counterfactual. We cannot say how long the same work would have taken with opaque formats, no fixpoint, or without a model.
  • Selection. The documented bugs are the ones that were interesting; the sample size is nine.
  • Dates are not effort. “Same day” means a commit the same day.
  • Co-author lines are not contribution. They show that a model was in the loop on 69% of commits, nothing more.
  • The models changed during the project, and some results (and the capabilities behind them) will not transfer to other models or to later ones.
  • Language is small by design (no type classes in the core). Part of the brevity is the language, not the method.

9. Conclusion

Looking back over the bugs we hit and the one backend we failed to build directly, two things stand out. First, an oracle: a fixpoint test, byte-identical output between two generators, a fixed set of benchmarks with known results. Wherever such an oracle existed, errors surfaced quickly and were fixed the same day; the long-lived bugs sat in code paths that no program had yet exercised, and each new consumer of the compiler (a REPL, a Haskell front end, an ABC optimiser) found some. Readable intermediate formats made diagnosis fast, but it was the oracle that told us something was wrong. Second, a decomposition chosen by a human. Our direct attempts at a WebAssembly generator, starting from bytecode, stalled; the approach worked after the problem was split into a C generator from Retag, a compilation of that C with Emscripten, and then a direct generator checked against that result. Within each step the model did the work well; choosing the steps, so that each one produced something readable and checkable, was the part we supplied. We do not know whether a model, given enough compute and no guidance, would find such a decomposition itself, and we have not tested it. For now the useful form of human steering seems to be less about writing code and more about building oracles and choosing the order of intermediate stages. That balance may well shift.
